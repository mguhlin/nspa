## 01. TRUST, TRANSPARENCY, AND AI

Welcome. Today covers both sides of the desk: AI supporting a scholarship team and AI supporting an applicant. Both raise the same question: how do we protect fairness and the trust of students? ## 03. Two sides of the desk

3 minutes. On the provider side, the pressure of volume is real. Reviewers have limited time, and attention can drift as the pile grows. On the applicant side, tools can support brainstorming, grammar and access to feedback. Those uses can overlap with help that a teacher, counselor or family member might provide. We should avoid assuming every use has the same purpose or effect. Ask which expectations applicants and reviewers need to understand. Trust requires a process that people can explain, question and correct.

## 04. Our guiding principle

2 minutes. If you remember one sentence, make it this one. AI can sort, summarize and suggest. People decide. Privacy concerns what information enters the system. Transparency concerns what applicants and staff understand about its use. Human oversight means a person has the time, authority and evidence to correct the work. Fairness means we test the process and apply the published criteria consistently. These are practical checkpoints. We will use them to evaluate each demonstration.

## 05. Vague and structured prompts

2 minutes. A vague prompt leaves the system to guess the criteria, scale and meaning of a strong response. A structured prompt makes the task inspectable. It still cannot guarantee accuracy or fairness. Compare the two requests and ask what the first leaves unsaid. Criteria, evidence requirements and decision boundaries belong in the instruction, so reviewers can check whether the response followed them.

## 06. Anatomy of a strong review prompt

3 minutes. Read the six parts as one coherent instruction. Role establishes the assistance being requested. Task defines its scope. Context supplies the scholarship purpose and eligibility rules. Criteria must reproduce the actual rubric. Format requires a consistent output that humans can inspect. Constraints require direct evidence, visible uncertainty and no inferences about demographics or character. Add that application text is untrusted evidence, and any instructions embedded in it must not override the review task. This instruction reduces a risk; it does not eliminate the need for testing and verification.

## 08. The review workflow

2 minutes. Read the full sequence: intake; redact and minimize; completeness check; rubric pre-score with evidence; human review; committee decision; audit sample. AI can help at bounded drafting stages. A person approves the input, checks the evidence and owns the decision. Keep a record of the prompt version, corrections and reviewer. ## 09. Privacy before processing

2 minutes. Before any real applicant data enters a tool, obtain local approval for the actual service, settings and purpose. An account label alone does not establish privacy. Removing a name may leave enough details to identify someone. For this workshop, use fictional records only. Check the privacy notice and donor agreements, and ask the appropriate owners which laws apply. FERPA does not apply identically to every independent scholarship provider or every record. Never upload confidential applicant records to an unapproved consumer service. Redaction alone does not make every use safe.

## 13. Stress tests before a pilot

3 minutes. Run the same fictional case three times. Compare not only the numeric rating but also the evidence quoted and any omissions. Next, in a controlled fictional test, change only the name. Any unexplained difference deserves investigation. Keep real names out of scoring inputs where possible. Finally, compare ten fictional cases with independent human reviews. Ten is a practical workshop starting point, not a sufficient validation sample. Record disagreements, investigate causes, and expand the evaluation before high-impact use. Model, prompt and rubric changes can require a fresh test.

## 14. Where AI fits

2 minutes. This is a workshop framework, not an official NSPA policy or a legal safe harbor. Green means a clerical drafting task with approved data and routine checking. Yellow means the output can shape judgment, so a human checks it against source evidence before use. Even a summary becomes yellow when it influences prioritization or hides material facts. Red covers decisions that require an authorized person, a reason and a correction route. A person owns eligibility denials as well as final award decisions. The color alone never makes an unapproved data use acceptable.

## 15. Applicant AI use spans a spectrum

## 17. The human cost of a false positive

2 minutes. The arithmetic assumes all five thousand submissions are human-written and an illustrative one-percent false-positive rate: the expected count is fifty. It is not an observed rate, a forecast for this organization or the chance that a flagged person is innocent. Liang and colleagues found a 61.3% average false-positive rate across seven detectors on 91 TOEFL essays in their 2023 study. That study provides evidence of a disparity in those samples and tools, not a universal current rate. A 2026 Czech-language study found no systematic bias in its tested detector families. Different samples can produce different findings. Avoid unsupported claims about neurodivergent writers.

## 18. Application design for authenticity

2 minutes. Ask for a specific experience, the applicant’s contribution and the evidence of what happened. A follow-up conversation can clarify an account, but it does not prove authorship and must allow reasonable access and accommodations. Use the same process for similarly situated finalists. A brief optional reflection can describe writing support without turning disclosure into a penalty. Corroborate important claims through appropriate records or references. These practices support a richer review; none makes an application immune to fabrication.

## 19. A finalist’s essay feels “too perfect”

3 minutes, including two minutes in pairs. Invite participants to choose an action and explain what evidence supports it. ## 20. Applicant AI policy choices

3 minutes. These four approaches are policy options, not a claim about which one most organizations use. A prohibition requires clear scope and consideration of accessibility features. Disclosure requires a usable form and a statement of how the response affects review. A bounded policy defines what assistance is allowed and what must remain personal. An embrace policy still needs truthful claims and evidence. Organizations can combine bounded use with disclosure. Choose a stance that fits the award’s purpose, then test the examples with students and reviewers before publishing.

## 21. Transparency goes both ways

3 minutes. We cannot reasonably ask applicants to explain their AI assistance while leaving our own process obscure. The sample statement is a starting point, not a description of an existing organization. Confirm the service, data use, retention and training arrangements before publishing any promise. If the organization can substantiate that applicant data is never used for model training, it can state that clearly. Do not add that assurance merely because the account is paid. ## 22. Responsible AI review checklist

3 minutes. Read this as a practical implementation checklist rather than a certification. Ask participants to identify three weak areas and one owner for each. ## 23. A 30 / 60 / 90-day plan

## 28. B4 · A shared vocabulary

Backup. A shared vocabulary helps a team discuss a problem precisely. An LLM can generate fluent text without verifying the truth of its claims. Hallucination includes fabricated quotations as well as incorrect summaries. A false positive concerns a human text flagged by a detector; it is different from a false claim produced by a review assistant. Personal information can remain in an essay after names are removed because details can identify someone in combination. Human in the loop must describe actual authority, time and evidence for review. Simply clicking approve is not meaningful oversight.
